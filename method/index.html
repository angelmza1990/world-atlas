World Atlas

Method

Where the figures come from, how continents are built, and what to keep in mind when reading them.

Sources

  • Indicators: the World Bank's World Development Indicators, licensed CC BY 4.0, for the 217 economies the World Bank reports on, from 1960. The table below lists each series and its code.
  • Country facts (official names, capitals, languages, currencies, land borders, subregions): the open mledoze/countries dataset, licensed ODbL.
  • Borders on the maps: Natural Earth, 1:50 million, through the world-atlas TopoJSON package. Boundaries on the maps do not imply any position on disputed territory.
  • Flags: flagcdn.com, public domain.

Every source file is kept in the repository under data/raw/, and a scheduled job refreshes them every week; the World Bank publishes updates a few times a year.

Continents

Countries are grouped into six continents by their region in the countries dataset: Africa, Asia, Europe, Oceania, and the Americas split into South America and North America, which includes Central America and the Caribbean. Russia counts in Europe and Turkey in Asia, as in the source. These differ from the World Bank's own regions (such as "Europe & Central Asia"), which each country page also shows.

Continent and world figures are computed from their countries:

  • Totals (population, GDP, area, net migration) add the countries up.
  • Rates (birth and death rates, fertility, life expectancy, age shares, urbanization) are averages weighted by population, so India counts more than Iceland.
  • Per-person ratios divide two totals: GDP per capita is total GDP over total population; density is total population over total land area.

A continent's figure for a year is published only when the countries with data hold at least 90% of its population, so a gap in reporting never looks like a change. Indicators that do not add up meaningfully (GDP growth, inflation, unemployment, inequality) are shown by country only.

Reading the figures

  • Birth and death rates are per 1,000 people per year. Fertility is the average number of children a woman would have at current rates; about 2.1 replaces a population without migration.
  • GDP is in current US dollars at market exchange rates, which swing with currencies. PPP figures adjust for price levels and are better for comparing living standards.
  • Density uses land area, excluding inland water; surface area includes it.
  • Population pyramids come from five-year age groups by sex; the last group is everyone aged 80 and over.
  • The most recent years of many series are World Bank estimates and are revised as censuses and national accounts come in.
  • Maps, rankings and charts use a country's figure for the chosen year, or its most recent one from the four years before when that year is missing, and say so.

Reproduce it

The code is on GitHub. scripts/fetch_data.py downloads the sources and scripts/build_data.py builds the files the pages read. The build checks that the countries add up to the World Bank's world population and stops otherwise.
